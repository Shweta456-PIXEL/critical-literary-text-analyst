# Critical Analysis Report: "The Salt Ledger"

> **ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE.** This report analyses the invented passage in `sample_input.md`. It demonstrates the report format from `references/output_format.md`. It is not an analysis of a real literary work, and the context statements are prompts for verification, not findings.

## 1. Text and scope

- **Text:** "The Salt Ledger" (fictional, 2026, written for illustration)
- **Genre / form:** short prose fiction, third-person narration
- **Passage analysed:** para. 1-2 (about 160 words)
- **Copyright status:** original fictional text
- **Unit of analysis and its limit:** a single short passage. The analysis cannot support claims about a whole story, an author or a period.

## 2. Research focus and assumptions

The researcher is interested in land, naming and records, and has not chosen a theory. **Assumptions:** the passage is read in its original English; the setting is a coastal salt-making community; the passage never names an empire or a government, so any colonial reading is an *inference* from "the English script", the survey and the taxation logic.

## 3. Method summary

Two close readings of the passage produced nine observations. Observations were coded openly, clustered into three themes, and tested for counter-evidence. A theoretical lens was then chosen because it explained the coded patterns better than a plain reading. Research questions were generated at four scales and checked against six criteria. No computational tool was used.

## 6. Themes and evidence

### Theme 1: Surveying and naming convert a shared ecological commons into administrable, taxable property

**Confidence:** strong

| ID | Quotation | Location | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | "numbered one through forty" | para. 1, s.3 | Counting imposes a grid on pans that previously had no individual owner. |
| E2 | "what is entered can be taxed" | para. 2, s.2 | Savitri states the logic: recording leads to taxation. |
| E3 | "a book of names" | para. 1, s.2 | Naming is carried like equipment, as part of the survey. |

**Counter-evidence / ambiguity:** "belonged to no one and to everyone" could be read as narratorial nostalgia for an unowned commons, or as a sober statement that community use had no formal title and was therefore vulnerable. The passage does not settle this.

### Theme 2: The text sets written records against knotted, oral and ecological records and gives the latter their own authority

**Confidence:** plausible

| ID | Quotation | Location | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| E4 | "debts were kept as knots in a red thread" | para. 2, s.3 | The family has a working accounting system that is not text. |
| E5 | "the water remembers him differently" | para. 2, s.4 | Memory is attributed to water, so the surveyor's record is not the only one. |
| E6 | "tied one more knot for the surveyor" | para. 2, s.3 | Savitri enters the surveyor into her ledger: the system answers the survey with its own entry. |

**Counter-evidence / ambiguity:** the knot is "small and tight", which can read as a private, limited gesture rather than effective resistance. The text does not show the knot having any effect on the survey.

### Theme 3: The environment acts beyond human inscription

**Confidence:** plausible

| ID | Quotation | Location | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| E7 | "the tide went on rewriting the shoreline" | para. 2, s.5 | Writing vocabulary (rewrite) is used for the tide, linking nature to record-making. |
| E8 | "white and cracked like old plates" | para. 1, s.1 | The pans appear fragile and worn before the survey, so the land is already a made, used landscape. |
| E9 | "without asking anyone's permission" | para. 2, s.5 | The tide ignores permission, in contrast to the survey, which depends on authority. |

**Counter-evidence / ambiguity:** the tide "rewriting" could be read as equating natural change with the surveyor's change, which might naturalise the dispossession. A close reader should weigh both readings.

## 7. Theoretical interpretation

**Lens:** postcolonial theory (epistemic violence and the colonial archive), with ecocriticism as a secondary lens.

**Why this lens:** the coded patterns (renaming, surveying, taxation, an unreadable English script, competing records) match the guiding questions in `references/theoretical_lenses.md` for postcolonialism and epistemic violence. The ecocritical lens is added because water, tides and land carry much of the meaning. **Caution:** the passage names no empire or government. "English script" is the only explicit marker, so the colonial reading is plausible, not certain.

**Reading:** the passage dramatises a shift from relational to administrative knowledge of land. Counting, naming and entering [E1, E2, E3] turn a used commons into something that can be assessed and taxed. The community's own knowledge is not erased in the passage but is made *illegible to the official record*: the creek has a name that does not appear on the map [O2], and the family ledger is not the kind of document the survey recognises [E4]. This is a textual instance of what Spivak's notion of epistemic violence helps to describe: not violence against bodies, but the delegitimising of a system of knowing (the researcher should consult the original essays and check the exact formulation). Savitri's reply, that water "remembers him differently" [E5], is the passage's counter-historiographic move: it names a rival archive. The ecocritical lens adds that this archive is partly non-human: the tide continues writing [E7, E9], so the landscape cannot be fully fixed by the map.

**Complication:** the knot is small and the tide's agency may seem to naturalise loss (Theme 2 and 3 counter-evidence). The passage may therefore be read as less hopeful than the "counter-archive" reading suggests.

**Confidence:** plausible for the combined reading; strong for the narrower claim that the passage contrasts written and knotted records.

**What this lens makes visible:** the politics of naming and recording; the link between ecology and administration. **What it hides:** Savitri's inner life (she is never shown thinking), the passage's formal features such as its aphoristic dialogue, and non-colonial readings (for example a purely local land dispute).

## 9. DH workflow suggestions

1. **Annotation scheme:** a spreadsheet with columns for passage ID, location, code, record-keeping form (written / numerical / knotted / oral / ecological), who controls it, and confidence.
2. **Corpus design (for RQ4):** 20-30 short stories; log source, edition, language and licence for each; keep full texts out of the public repository unless licensing is clear.
3. **Tool suggestions:** a concordance tool (AntConc or Voyant) for naming and counting words; TEI-lite markup if the project grows toward a digital edition.
4. **What methods cannot do:** concordance counts will not capture irony, silence or the force of Savitri's final knot; topic modelling is not suitable for a single 160-word passage.
5. **Validation:** double-code 10-20% of passages with a peer and discuss disagreements.

## 10. Reflexivity, limitations and verification

- **Reflexivity note:** the researcher reads English and Hindi and is new to postcolonial theory; this may favour readings drawn from widely taught English-language theory and underweight regional critical traditions. Regional scholarship should be consulted before any claim about a specific coastal culture.
- **Limitations:** (1) one short passage; (2) invented text with no biography, edition or reception to consult; (3) colonial context inferred from limited markers; (4) LLM-assisted analysis can over-order ambiguity and reflect a canonical critical vocabulary.

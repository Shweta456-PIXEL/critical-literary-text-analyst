# Theoretical Lenses

A selection guide and short profiles of lenses that are widely used in literary and cultural analysis within the Digital Humanities and Social Sciences. Use these as **starting orientations**; always advise the user to read the primary works, because the summaries here are simplified.

## 1. How to choose a lens

1. Start from **evidence**: what patterns did coding reveal (naming, land, bodies, money, silence, emotion)?
2. Ask which lens **explains the pattern better** than a plain reading.
3. Check the **fit of context**: does the text's historical and cultural setting make this lens meaningful, or are you imposing it?
4. Choose **one primary lens** and, at most, one secondary lens for a short analysis.
5. State **what the lens hides**. Every lens foregrounds some things (for example, power) and backgrounds others (for example, form or pleasure).
6. Where the lens originates in a different cultural context from the text, say so and consider local or regional critical traditions.

## 2. Postcolonialism

**Core idea.** Examines how colonial power shaped, and continues to shape, cultures, languages, knowledge and representation, and how texts resist, negotiate or reproduce those relations.

**Key concepts.** Orientalism and othering (Said); hybridity, mimicry, ambivalence (Bhabha); the subaltern (Spivak, Subaltern Studies); writing back; decolonising the mind and language politics (Ngũgĩ); the colonial archive.

**Guiding questions.**
- Who names, maps, counts and classifies? Who is named?
- Which languages appear, and which are translated, glossed or left untranslated?
- How are land, labour and law represented?
- Does the text reproduce colonial categories or unsettle them?
- Whose perspective frames "development", "order" or "progress"?

**Evidence cues.** Naming and renaming; surveys, censuses, maps; glossaries; code-switching; contrasts between official and vernacular records; mimicry; spatial division.

**Cautions.** Avoid treating "colonial" as a single experience; attend to regional, caste, class and gender differences. Avoid applying Euro-American postcolonial theory without considering regional critique. Avoid presenting colonised characters only as victims.

## 3. Epistemic violence

**Core idea.** Harm done through the destruction, delegitimising or silencing of ways of knowing, speaking or being heard (term associated with Spivak; related to Fricker's work on epistemic injustice).

**Guiding questions.**
- Whose knowledge counts as knowledge in the text (written, official, scientific) and whose is dismissed (oral, embodied, ecological, ritual)?
- Who is allowed to testify? Who is spoken for or translated by others?
- Are there instances of silencing, unreadable documents, mistranslation?
- Does the text itself preserve alternative knowledge forms?

**Evidence cues.** Dismissive labels ("superstition", "hearsay"), mistranslation, forced renaming, loss of records, unreliable intermediaries, characters unable to read the controlling script.

**Cautions.** Distinguish *epistemic violence* (about knowledge systems) from physical violence; do not romanticise the oppressed as holders of pure alternative knowledge; note that "the subaltern cannot speak" is a claim about the conditions of being heard, not a claim that people are literally silent.

## 4. Counter-historiography and subaltern history

**Core idea.** Reads texts as sites where official histories can be challenged, supplemented or reversed, bringing forward people, events and temporalities absent from dominant narratives. Related to history from below, Subaltern Studies, oral history, "reading along the archival grain" versus "against the grain" (Stoler; Guha), and critical fabulation (Hartman).

**Guiding questions.**
- What event or period does the text revisit, and from whose position?
- What does the text claim the official record omitted?
- How does the text handle evidence: documents, rumours, memories, songs?
- Does it propose alternative chronologies (cyclical, ancestral, ecological)?
- What is the status of the fictional invention: does it fill gaps openly or pose as fact?

**Evidence cues.** Competing accounts of the same event; documents that are cited and contradicted; oral sources; "official" dates versus remembered ones; use of lists, ledgers or records as narrative devices.

**Cautions.** Fiction is not evidence for historical claims; separate "what the text imagines" from "what the archive shows". Check for the risk of replacing one totalising narrative with another.

## 5. Ecocriticism

**Core idea.** Studies relationships between literature and the physical environment, including representation of nature, place, animals, climate, extraction and ecological justice.

**Key strands.** Place-based criticism; environmental justice and postcolonial ecocriticism (Nixon's "slow violence"); material ecocriticism (agency of matter); Anthropocene and climate fiction criticism; animal studies; blue/oceanic humanities.

**Guiding questions.**
- Is the environment a backdrop, an agent or a character?
- How are land and water owned, measured or exploited?
- Who bears environmental harm, and who is credited with environmental knowledge?
- Which temporal scales appear (tidal, seasonal, generational, geological)?
- Does the text contrast extractive and relational understandings of nature?

**Evidence cues.** Weather, tides, seasons; resource vocabulary (pans, forests, coal); embodied labour; pollution; species names (local versus scientific).

**Cautions.** Avoid romanticised "pure nature" or "ecological Indian"-type stereotypes; avoid ignoring human communities; consider environmental justice, not only conservation.

## 6. Intersectionality

**Core idea.** Analyses how overlapping systems of power (gender, caste, class, race, religion, sexuality, disability, region, citizenship) produce distinct experiences that cannot be explained by any single category (Crenshaw; Collins; also Dalit feminist and Black feminist thought).

**Guiding questions.**
- Which social categories are marked in the text, and which are left unmarked (and so treated as default)?
- How do characters' positions combine (for example a lower-caste woman labourer)?
- How do institutions (law, family, employer, state) treat these intersections?
- Whose experience is generalised as "women's" or "workers'" experience?

**Evidence cues.** Naming and non-naming of caste, race or religion; occupational detail; domestic versus public space; differential access to literacy, land, mobility; speech varieties.

**Cautions.** Do not treat intersectionality as a list of identities; focus on structures and relations. Avoid assuming categories from a character's name or appearance without textual evidence. Be careful with transposing US-origin categories to other contexts without discussing local formations (for example caste in South Asia).

## 7. Cultural materialism and New Historicism

**Core idea.** Reads texts as embedded in material conditions and power relations: publishing, patronage, censorship, labour, class and institutions. Cultural materialism (Williams; Dollimore and Sinfield) emphasises political commitment and contemporary relevance; New Historicism (Greenblatt, Gallagher) emphasises the circulation of social energy between literary and non-literary texts.

**Guiding questions.**
- Under what material conditions was the text produced, published and read?
- Which non-literary documents (laws, maps, advertisements, reports, petitions) form its context?
- How does the text negotiate or contest dominant ideology?
- What forms of dissidence or containment can be seen?

**Evidence cues.** References to markets, wages, taxes, regulations, print culture; paratext; reception history; links to contemporaneous documents the user can supply.

**Cautions.** Do not assume context the user has not supplied or verified; avoid reducing literary form to an "effect" of economics.

## 8. Affect theory

**Core idea.** Attends to feelings, moods, intensities and bodily responses as social and political forces rather than merely private emotions (Sedgwick, Ahmed, Berlant, Massumi, Cvetkovich).

**Key concepts.** Affective economies and "sticky" objects (Ahmed); cruel optimism and impasse (Berlant); ordinary affects (Stewart); public feelings and archives of feeling (Cvetkovich); reparative versus paranoid reading (Sedgwick).

**Guiding questions.**
- What moods or atmospheres does the text produce, and through which formal means (rhythm, imagery, pacing, syntax)?
- Which emotions circulate between characters, objects and places?
- How do feelings align people with or against social norms?
- What do the text's silences or restraint feel like?

**Evidence cues.** Bodily descriptions; repetition and rhythm; atmosphere; objects charged with feeling; shifts in tempo; understatement.

**Cautions.** Emotional responses of the researcher are data but not proof; separate "the text produces..." from "I feel...". A language model has no feelings; do not present its reading as an experienced response.

## 9. Feminist and gender criticism

**Core idea.** Examines representations of gender, the politics of authorship and canon formation, and the textual construction of femininity, masculinity and non-binary identities.

**Guiding questions.** Who speaks and who is spoken for? How is domestic and reproductive labour represented? How does form (voice, genre) carry gendered meaning? What is the canon's treatment of women writers?

**Cautions.** Avoid essentialising "women's writing"; combine with intersectional analysis where evidence suggests.

## 10. Queer theory

**Core idea.** Questions normative assumptions about gender, sexuality and kinship, and reads for non-normative desire, temporality and form (Butler, Sedgwick, Halberstam, Muñoz).

**Guiding questions.** Which norms govern the text's world? Are there ambiguities in desire, kinship, or time that resist normativity? What does the text leave unsaid, and what is the politics of that silence?

**Cautions.** Avoid retrospective labelling of historical figures without evidence and care; consider local histories and terms.

## 11. Marxist and political-economy criticism

**Core idea.** Reads texts in relation to class, labour, property and the circulation of capital (Jameson, Eagleton, Williams, Moretti on world literature).

**Guiding questions.** How are work, wages, debt, land and commodities represented? Who profits and who bears risk? What contradictions does the form attempt to resolve?

**Cautions.** Avoid determinism; consider cultural and symbolic agency.

## 12. Narratology and reader-response

**Narratology** (Genette, Bal, Chatman): focalisation, narrative levels, time order, duration, frequency, narrator reliability. Useful as a *descriptive* base for any other lens.

**Reader-response** (Iser, Fish, Rosenblatt): how meaning arises in the act of reading; consider the implied reader and interpretive communities.

**Cautions.** Narratological description is not interpretation by itself; connect form to meaning.

## 13. Digital and computational lenses

Useful when the project has a DH dimension.

- **Distant reading** (Moretti): patterns across many texts; use with close reading for validation.
- **Algorithmic criticism** (Ramsay): treats computational output as a provocation for interpretation.
- **Critical digital humanities and data feminism**: ask who built the corpus, what was digitised and what was left out, how OCR and tokenisation affect minority languages.
- **Minimal computing and postcolonial DH**: attention to infrastructure and language inequality.

**Cautions.** A quantitative pattern needs interpretation; digitisation creates its own biases (canon, language, scanning quality).

## 14. Combining lenses

Good pairings when evidence supports them:

| Pairing | Fits when the text... |
|---|---|
| Postcolonial + ecocritical | links colonial administration to land, water or resource control |
| Postcolonial + epistemic violence | dramatises silencing, mistranslation or erasure of local knowledge |
| Intersectionality + cultural materialism | shows how labour, caste/race and gender combine under specific economic conditions |
| Affect theory + counter-historiography | uses feeling, rumour or memory to carry history that archives omit |
| Ecocritical + cultural materialist | treats ecology as a product of production and distribution systems |
| Narratology + any lens | needs formal grounding for a theoretical claim |

When combining, say which lens leads, and avoid stacking terms without showing what each adds.

## 15. Lens fit table

| If the coding shows... | Consider |
|---|---|
| Renaming, mapping, surveying, language hierarchy | Postcolonialism, epistemic violence |
| Records, ledgers, rumours, competing accounts of the past | Counter-historiography |
| Land, water, weather, extraction | Ecocriticism |
| Overlapping disadvantages by caste/class/gender/race | Intersectionality |
| Wages, taxes, publication, patronage, censorship | Cultural materialism, political economy |
| Atmosphere, bodily reaction, restraint, repetition | Affect theory |
| Gendered labour and voice | Feminist criticism |
| Non-normative desire or kinship | Queer theory |
| Narrator, perspective, time structure | Narratology |
| Large corpora and patterns across texts | Distant reading with critical DH |

**Reminder:** summarise the lens in your own words, give the evidence, and encourage the user to consult primary theoretical texts instead of relying on this file or on Claude for citation details.
